Summary of the DICE-2023 Core Model (DICEModel.jl)

Source: src/CoreModel.jl in DICEModel.jl (MIT license). This is a regionalized implementation of DICE-2023 in Julia/JuMP, solved with Ipopt. Numeric parameter values live in the DICEParameters struct (src/Parameters.jl); the values used by the two main scenarios are tabulated in Section 9.

Hover over (or tap) any symbol in an equation to see what it means and whether it is a variable or a parameter.

1 Overview

DICE is an optimal growth model coupled to a simple climate model. A social planner chooses, for each period \(t\) and region \(r\):

  • the emission control rate \(\mu_{t,r}\) (MIU_R, bounded by \(0 \le \mu \le \bar\mu_{t,r}\)),
  • the savings rate \(s_{t,r}\) (S_R, \(0 \le s \le 1\)),
  • the carbon-cycle decay scaling \(\alpha_t\) (ALPHA, \(0.1 \le \alpha \le 100\)), which is pinned down by an equation rather than being a true policy choice.

The planner maximizes discounted, population-weighted welfare subject to the economy, emissions, carbon cycle, and temperature equations below. Time steps are of length \(\Delta\) (tstep) years, indexed \(t = 1,\dots,T\); regions are indexed \(r\).

2 Notation

Symbols are grouped by the section where they first appear. The Unit column gives units stated in the comments of the source code (CoreModel.jl). Units marked “implied” are not commented in the source but follow directly from the equations, and “not stated” means neither applies. Dollar amounts are in 2019 US dollars.

3 Economy

Gross output (Cobb-Douglas, labor \(L\) in millions, capital \(K\) in trillion USD):

\[ Y^{gross}_{t,r} = A_{t,r}\left(\frac{L_{t,r}}{1000}\right)^{1-\gamma_r} K_{t,r}^{\gamma_r} \]

Damages are a function of atmospheric warming \(T^{AT}_t\):

\[ D_{t,r} = a_{1,r}\,T^{AT}_t + a_{2,r}\,(T^{AT}_t)^{a_{3,r}}, \qquad \text{Damages}_{t,r} = Y^{gross}_{t,r}\, D_{t,r} \]

Output net of damages, and abatement cost:

\[ Y^{net}_{t,r} = Y^{gross}_{t,r}\,(1 - D_{t,r}), \qquad \Lambda_{t,r} = Y^{gross}_{t,r}\; \kappa_{t,r}\;\mu_{t,r}^{\,\theta_{t,r}} \]

Net output, investment, consumption:

\[ Y_{t,r} = Y^{net}_{t,r} - \Lambda_{t,r}, \qquad I_{t,r} = s_{t,r}\,Y_{t,r}, \qquad C_{t,r} = Y_{t,r} - I_{t,r} \]

Capital accumulation (with initial stock \(K_{1,r} = k_{0,r}\), and a lower bound \(K \ge 1\)):

\[ K_{t,r} \le (1-\delta_r)^{\Delta} K_{t-1,r} + \Delta\, I_{t-1,r} \]

Per capita consumption (thousands of USD):

\[ c_{t,r} = \frac{1000\, C_{t,r}}{L_{t,r}} \]

Implied carbon price from the marginal abatement cost:

\[ P^{C}_{t,r} = p^{back}_{t,r}\;\mu_{t,r}^{\,\theta_{t,r}-1} \]

Global aggregates (\(Y\), \(C\), \(I\), \(K\), damages, abatement cost) are sums over regions, and the global savings rate is \(S_t = I_t / Y_t\). In the last periods (\(t \ge 38\)) the global savings rate is fixed at \(S_t = 0.28\). Consumption must satisfy \(C_{t,r} \ge 2\) and \(c_{t,r} \ge 0.01\).

4 Emissions

With emissions intensity \(\sigma_{t,r}\) and exogenous land-use emissions \(E^{land}_{t,r}\):

\[ E^{ind}_{t,r} = \sigma_{t,r}\,Y^{gross}_{t,r}\,(1-\mu_{t,r}) \]

\[ E^{CO_2}_{t,r} = \left(\sigma_{t,r}\,Y^{gross}_{t,r} + E^{land}_{t,r}\right)(1-\mu_{t,r}) \]

\[ E^{CO_2e}_{t,r} = \left(\sigma_{t,r}\,Y^{gross}_{t,r} + E^{land}_{t,r} + E^{nonCO_2}_{t,r}\right)(1-\mu_{t,r}) \]

where \(E^{nonCO_2}_{t,r}\) is the baseline abatable non-CO\(_2\) GHG emissions (co2e_ghgabateb). Global emissions are regional sums. Cumulative CO\(_2\) emissions (GtCO\(_2\) converted to GtC):

\[ \text{CCATOT}_t = \text{CCATOT}_{t-1} + E^{CO_2}_{t-1}\,\frac{\Delta}{3.666} \]

5 Carbon cycle (four-reservoir impulse response)

What a reservoir is. A reservoir here is a mathematical pool that holds excess atmospheric CO\(_2\) and drains at its own rate. It is not a physical place such as “the ocean” or “the forest.” CO\(_2\) does not decay at a single rate after it is emitted: some is absorbed within years, some takes centuries to mix into the deep ocean, and some stays airborne for millennia. A single exponential cannot capture that spread, so DICE sums four exponentials. Pool \(j\) receives a fraction \(\phi_j\) of each emission (the \(\phi_j\) sum to 1) and drains with timescale \(\tau_j\). The pools describe how long carbon stays in the atmosphere, not where it goes. Carbon that leaves the atmosphere ends up in sinks, tracked separately as \(\text{CACC}_t\).

Figure 1 shows what the four pools do after a single pulse. The fast pools empty quickly, so the total drops steeply at first, then declines only slowly because the slow pools dominate what remains. In this example, more than a third of the pulse is still airborne after 500 years. This is why emissions have effects lasting centuries.

Each period, every pool loses a fraction of its old contents and gains a share of new emissions (the equation below). The scaling factor \(\alpha_t\) multiplies every timescale: when \(\alpha_t > 1\) all pools drain more slowly, and the model raises \(\alpha_t\) as sinks fill and the planet warms.

Each reservoir \(j \in \{0,1,2,3\}\) has an emission share \(\phi_j\) and a timescale \(\tau_j\), with timescales stretched by \(\alpha_t\). Emissions are converted to GtC by dividing by 3.667:

\[ R_{j,t} = \phi_j\,\tau_j\,\alpha_t \,\frac{E^{CO_2}_t}{3.667}\left(1 - e^{-\Delta/(\tau_j\alpha_t)}\right) + R_{j,t-1}\,e^{-\Delta/(\tau_j\alpha_t)} \]

Atmospheric carbon is the preindustrial equilibrium plus the reservoir sum:

\[ M^{AT}_t = M^{eq} + R_{0,t} + R_{1,t} + R_{2,t} + R_{3,t}, \qquad M^{AT}_t \ge 10 \]

Carbon in sinks (ocean and other):

\[ \text{CACC}_t = \text{CCATOT}_t - \left(M^{AT}_t - M^{eq}\right) \]

The scaling factor \(\alpha_t\) is determined by requiring that the 100-year integrated impulse response (IRF100) computed from the reservoirs equals a linear function of accumulated sink carbon and temperature:

\[ \underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{3}\alpha_t\,\phi_j\,\tau_j\left(1 - e^{-100/(\alpha_t \tau_j)}\right)}_{\text{IRF100}_t} = \mathit{irf}_0 + \mathit{irc}\cdot \text{CACC}_t + \mathit{irt}\cdot T^{AT}_t \]

This makes carbon uptake slow down as sinks fill and as the planet warms.

6 Climate

Radiative forcing from CO\(_2\), exogenous other forcing, and the abatable non-CO\(_2\) GHG forcing:

\[ F_t = F_{2\times}\,\frac{\ln(M^{AT}_t / M^{eq})}{\ln 2} + F^{misc}_t + \sum_r F^{GHG}_{t,r} \]

Abatable non-CO\(_2\) forcing evolves with persistence \(f_2\) and an emission response \(f_1\):

\[ F^{GHG}_{t,r} = f_2\,F^{GHG}_{t-1,r} + f_1\,E^{nonCO_2}_{t-1,r}\,(1-\mu_{t-1,r}) \]

Two-box temperature model (fast and slow boxes, time constants \(d_1, d_2\) and equilibrium responses \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\)):

\[ T^{box}_{k,t} = T^{box}_{k,t-1}\,e^{-\Delta/d_k} + \lambda_k\,F_t\left(1 - e^{-\Delta/d_k}\right), \qquad k = 1,2 \]

\[ T^{AT}_t = T^{box}_{1,t} + T^{box}_{2,t}, \qquad 0.5 \le T^{AT}_t \le 20 \]

7 Welfare and objective

Period utility per capita (CRRA with elasticity \(\eta_r\) of marginal utility, elasmu):

\[ u_{t,r} = \frac{c_{t,r}^{\,1-\eta_r} - 1}{1-\eta_r} - 1 \]

Period welfare weights utility by population \(L\), the social discount factor \(\rho_{t,r}\) (rr), and normalized regional welfare weights \(w_r\). The source states that these weights are exogenous and are not Negishi weights:

\[ U_t = \sum_r u_{t,r}\; L_{t,r}\;\rho_{t,r}\;\frac{w_r}{\sum_{r'} w_{r'}} \]

Objective:

\[ \max_{\{\mu_{t,r},\, s_{t,r}\}} \quad W = \Delta \cdot \text{scale}_1 \sum_{t=1}^{T} U_t + \text{scale}_2 \]

8 Solution and post-processing

The problem is a nonlinear program solved with Ipopt (default up to 3000 iterations, tolerance \(10^{-8}\)). Optional user bounds can add scenario constraints of the form \(x_{t,r} \le, \ge, = v\) on any model variable, where \(v\) may be a scalar, a time vector, or a time-by-region matrix.

Derived outputs computed after solving:

  • Social cost of carbon (dollars per tCO\(_2\)), from the shadow prices of the emissions and consumption constraints: \[ \text{SCC}_{t,r} = -1000\,\frac{\nu^{E}_{t,r}}{\nu^{C}_{t,r}} \] where \(\nu^{E}_{t,r}\) and \(\nu^{C}_{t,r}\) are the shadow prices (Lagrange multipliers) on the emissions and consumption constraints. The factor 1000 converts units to dollars per tCO\(_2\). Why is the SCC a ratio of two shadow prices?
  • Global control rate: \(\mu_t = 1 - \dfrac{\sum_r E^{ind}_{t,r}}{\sum_r \sigma_{t,r}Y^{gross}_{t,r}}\)
  • Global damage fraction: \(\dfrac{\sum_r D_{t,r}}{\sum_r Y^{gross}_{t,r}}\) (as coded, this sums regional fractions in the numerator, not damages)
  • Atmospheric CO\(_2\) in ppm: \(M^{AT}_t / 2.13\)
  • Abatement cost share of output: \(\Lambda_t / Y_t\)
  • Airborne fractions relative to 2020 and 1765: \(\dfrac{M^{AT}_t - M_0}{\text{CCATOT}_t - \text{cum}_0}\) and \(\dfrac{M^{AT}_t - M^{eq}}{\text{CCATOT}_t}\)
  • Endogenous interest rates (long and short) from the growth of per capita consumption and the discount factor.
NoteWhy is the SCC a ratio of two shadow prices?

The two constraints. The emissions constraint is the equation that defines regional CO\(_2\) emissions, \(E^{CO_2}_{t,r} = (\sigma_{t,r}Y^{gross}_{t,r} + E^{land}_{t,r})(1-\mu_{t,r})\). The consumption constraint is the accounting rule \(C_{t,r} = Y_{t,r} - I_{t,r}\).

What a Lagrange multiplier is. Each constraint has a Lagrange multiplier, also called a shadow price or dual value. It answers one question: if this constraint were relaxed by one unit, how much would the best achievable welfare change? For example, if you maximize happiness subject to a $100 budget and a 101st dollar would raise the best achievable happiness by 0.3, the multiplier on the budget constraint is 0.3.

Two experiments. The two multipliers correspond to two “what if” questions, shown below.

  • Relax the emissions constraint so that emissions are one GtCO\(_2\) higher than the economy would otherwise produce. The extra carbon warms the climate and raises damages, so welfare falls. The multiplier \(\nu^{E}\) is therefore negative, in welfare units per GtCO\(_2\).
  • Relax the consumption constraint so there is one more trillion USD of consumption. Welfare rises. The multiplier \(\nu^{C}\) is positive, in welfare units per trillion USD.

Why the ratio. Dividing one multiplier by the other cancels the welfare units, which have no meaning you can act on. What remains is trillion USD per GtCO\(_2\): the amount of consumption that would leave society exactly as well off as one more tonne of CO\(_2\) would make it worse off. The factor 1000 converts trillion USD per GtCO\(_2\) into USD per tCO\(_2\), the minus sign makes the SCC positive, and the code adds 0.00001 to the denominator to avoid dividing by zero.

Caveats. The signs depend on how the solver (Ipopt through JuMP) reports duals for a maximization problem; the formula’s leading minus sign assumes \(\nu^{E}\) comes out negative. The multipliers also include the welfare weights \(w_r\), the discount factor, and population, because those sit inside the objective.

Results from running the model

What differs: the temperature cap

cbopt t2c
Constraint on \(T^{AT}_t\) None (hard model bounds only, 0.5 to 20 °C) \(\le 2.0\) °C in all periods
Role of damages Set the level of abatement through the SCC Still counted in welfare, but the cap binds first
Emission control rate Chosen where marginal abatement cost equals the SCC Raised faster until the cap holds, within the ramp limit (0.55 in 2050 against 0.39 in cbopt)
Carbon price (CPRICE) About 115 USD/tCO\(_2\) in 2050 About 200 USD/tCO\(_2\) in 2050

Interpretation.

  • Because the parameters are identical, every difference in outcomes between the two scenarios is caused by the temperature cap alone. This makes the pair a clean comparison of “optimal” versus “2 °C-compatible” policy.
  • Temperature confirms the cap matters. cbopt warms to a peak of 2.6 °C (around 2110) and stays above 2 °C from about 2055 to 2175; t2c sits exactly at the 2.0 °C cap from 2075 to 2130 (the cap binds in 12 periods) and cools afterwards (Figure 2 shows the paths up to 2100).
  • Abatement is more front-loaded under the cap. Spending peaks at 3.2% of output in 2075 in t2c, against 3.0% in 2125 in cbopt. The ramp limit (delmiumax) is what makes early action necessary: a cap that is approached late cannot be met by cutting faster than 12 points per period.
  • In both scenarios the carbon price is within about 10% of the SCC in mid-century but far below it in 2025 (17 against 59 to 89 USD/tCO\(_2\)), because the ramp limit, not cost, holds the 2025 control rate at 0.10. Where the control rate hits its ceiling later, the two diverge again (543 against 914 USD/tCO\(_2\) in 2150 in cbopt).
  • The parameter choices are not neutral, and these results hold only for the defaults tabulated above. Scenarios such as altdam (a2base = 0.01) change the damage function; I have not run them.

10 Notes on the implementation

  • Several stocks (capital) use inequality constraints (<=) rather than equality; this is harmless in an optimum where investment is valuable.
  • The emissions unit conversion uses 3.667 in the reservoir equations but 3.666 in cumulative emissions.
  • Initial conditions for each reservoir, temperature box, and the stocks are set in period 1 from parameters.
  • The global damage fraction in post-processing is a sum of regional fractions over total gross output, which differs from the damage-weighted ratio \(\sum_r \text{Damages}_{t,r} / \sum_r Y^{gross}_{t,r}\).
